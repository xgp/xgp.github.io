The Loop: Why Nobody Admits They Had a Bad Time on the Riviera

Oct 3, 2026 · @xgp

Every morning in summer, a white ship the size of an apartment block would appear in the bay below my house in Villefranche-sur-Mer. By nine, its tenders were shuttling people to the quay. By ten, the old town was full of them, holding phones in front of the painted facades, queuing for a nineteen-euro Aperol Spritz, buying lavender sachets stitched in a factory nowhere near Provence. By four they were gone, and the quay was hosed down for the next ship.

I lived there for ten years. I grew up in Omaha, so I knew who these people were. Many of them were the people I grew up with: schoolteachers, insurance adjusters, retired nurses who had wanted to see the South of France since they were nineteen and had saved for the better part of a decade to do it. I watched them get what they came for. And I came to believe that almost none of them had a good time, that almost all of them said they did, and that this gap is not an accident but the engine that keeps the whole place running.

Boorstin's warning

In 1961 Daniel Boorstin published The Image, a book about how Americans had learned to prefer the representation of things to the things themselves. His chapter on travel argued that the traveler, an active person who went out into the world and was changed by it, had been replaced by the tourist, a passive consumer who waits for "attractions" to be delivered. The instrument of the change was the guidebook. Once Baedeker started putting asterisks next to the sights worth seeing, the order of experience inverted: the tourist no longer discovered what was interesting, he went to confirm what the book had already told him was interesting. The trip became a verification exercise. The starred sight was worth seeing because it was starred.

Boorstin wrote this before Tripadvisor, before Instagram, before the "Top 10 Hidden Gems of the Côte d'Azur" listicle. He would not have been surprised by any of it. What I want to add to his account is not the demand side, which he described perfectly, but what the demand does to the supply, and what the supply does back.

The hollowing

The tourist arrives with an image. The image has been assembled from films, paintings, novels, and now from ten thousand photographs of other people's holidays. It is specific: the striped umbrella, the pastis at dusk, the fisherman mending his nets, the old woman selling lavender. It is also, mostly, a picture of a place that stopped existing around 1965.

The locals know this. They also know that the tourist will pay for the image, not for the reality, and that the image is cheaper to produce. So they produce it. The fisherman's nets are decorative. The "Provençal" market stalls sell the same soaps as the ones in Nice, Antibes, and the Marseille airport. The restaurant on the quay serves a salade niçoise that no one who lives there would order, because everyone who lives there knows it is made from canned tuna and priced for people who will never come back.

Do this for one generation and you have a tourist town. Do it for three and something worse happens: the people who produce the image no longer have any relationship to the culture it depicts. They did not fish. They did not farm. They did not build the churches or paint the chapel. They inherited a reputation the way one inherits a house, and they have spent their lives renting out rooms in it. Their skill, and it is a real skill, is the same skill as the guidebook's: knowing which things the visitor expects to see and pointing at them. They are, quite literally, tour guides in a place they had no hand in making. A well-written guidebook can tell you as much as they can, and usually does.

The sociologist Erving Goffman, writing at almost exactly the same moment as Boorstin, described social life as theater, divided into a front stage, where the performance is given, and a back stage, where the performers drop the act, complain about the audience, and prepare the next show. Dean MacCannell later applied the idea to tourism and gave it a name: staged authenticity. The tourist, MacCannell argued, is not a fool who wants a fake. He is someone who sincerely wants to get backstage, to see how the locals really live, and the tourist industry's genius is to build a second front stage that looks like a back stage and sell tickets to it. The "authentic" fisherman's tavern is a set. The "family-run" olive mill has a gift shop where the family used to be. And the real back stage, the one the tourist actually wanted, recedes one room further every time a new set is built in front of it.

I lived in the real back stage, or close enough to it to hear what was said there. The disdain I heard from waiters, shopkeepers, and boat operators, sometimes muttered in French across the room, sometimes said to my face assuming I couldn’t understand, was not really contempt for the tourists. It was contempt for what they themselves had to become to serve them. There is no pride of ownership in selling a counterfeit of your grandfather's life. A person who does it every day for thirty years develops a low-grade hatred of the customer, of the product, and of the self. The tourist, meanwhile, is told he has experienced "the real Riviera" and has no way to know that the man who told him so despises him for believing it. He has been let backstage. He does not know it was a stage.

The flywheel

You would think a place that sells shoddy experiences at high prices would eventually lose its customers. The opposite happens, and Boorstin's guidebook is the reason.

Tourists do not choose destinations by sampling them. They choose by reputation, and reputation is now an industry. A town competes for the starred entry, the magazine spread, the influencer's sponsored week, the ranking on a booking platform whose algorithm rewards volume. Some of this is earned; a great deal of it is bought, either directly, through the familiar economy of hosted trips and placed content, or indirectly, through the sheer accumulated weight of prior visitors' photographs. Either way the effect is the same: attention concentrates. The places that already have visitors get more visitors, because the visitors are the advertisement.

More visitors means higher prices, and here the mechanism turns perverse. In a normal market high prices signal scarcity or quality. In the tourist market, high prices signal importance. The 38-euro salad on the Villefranche waterfront does not repel the visitor from Omaha; it confirms to him that he is somewhere that matters. He has paid a magnificent sum to be here, and the magnificent price of lunch is of a piece with that. Expense becomes evidence. The town that charges the most is, by the tourist's own logic, the town most worth visiting, and its ranking rises accordingly.

Two of Boorstin's insights combine here. The tourist seeks a caricature of the place, and he trusts the intermediaries to point him toward it. The intermediaries are paid by the caricature. There is no one in the chain whose interest is served by the tourist's actual satisfaction, because his actual satisfaction is never measured. What is measured is what he says afterward.

The testimony

Which brings me to the part I found hardest to watch, because I watched it happen to people I love.

Friends came to visit. They had wanted to see the Riviera their whole lives. They had told their colleagues, their book clubs, their churches. They had spent, in some cases, more than they earned in six months. They arrived, and I watched them have a mediocre time: bad food, rude service, crowds, heat, a museum that turned out to be two rooms, a "village" that was a gift shop with a parking problem (Èze). I watched their faces during the trip. And then I watched them go home and tell everyone it was the trip of a lifetime.

They were not lying in the ordinary sense. Something more interesting was happening, and psychology has a name for it. Leon Festinger called it cognitive dissonance: when what we have done and what we believe fail to line up, we revise the belief, because the deed cannot be undone. Elliot Aronson's experiments on effort justification showed the specific case. People who suffer to attain something rate it more highly than people who got it easily, not despite the suffering but because of it. The mind cannot hold "I spent a decade's savings and a lifetime of anticipation on this" alongside "this was not very good." One of the two has to go, and the money is already spent.

Add the social dimension and the pressure becomes overwhelming. My friends had not just told themselves the trip would be wonderful. They had told everyone they knew. To come back and report disappointment would mean admitting, in public, that the great project of their adult imaginative life had been a miscalculation. Nobody does this. So the review is five stars. The photographs are curated. The stories get better in the retelling. And the schoolteacher in the next classroom, hearing the stories, starts saving.

The loop closes

Put the three together and you have a machine with no off switch. The visitor's expectation, formed by the image, induces the locals to produce the image rather than the place. Producing the image hollows the place out and embitters the people, but it also concentrates reputation and raises prices, which the visitor reads as confirmation of importance. The visitor, having paid dearly in money and in public anticipation, cannot afford to be disappointed, so he reports rapture. His report becomes the next visitor's image. And around it goes.

This is my hypothesis, and I hold it with some conviction after a decade inside the machine: the great destinations of Europe are popular not primarily because people enjoy them, but because the structure of tourism makes it nearly impossible for anyone to say otherwise. Their popularity is a measurement of testimony, not of experience, and the testimony is systematically corrupted by the cost of producing it. A place can be, as I found Villefranche to be, mediocre, predatory, and spectacularly expensive, and remain a dream destination for decades, because the only people qualified to warn you are the ones who have most reason not to.

I want to concede the obvious objection before it is raised. Some people genuinely have a wonderful time. I have met them. The light on the water at twilight is real, the sea is clear, blue and warm, and a person who has never left Nebraska may be moved by the mere fact of standing on a Mediterranean quay in a way no amount of frozen bouillabaisse can spoil. I do not doubt any of this, and I am not claiming that every five-star review is a dissonance-reduction exercise. But the objection, though true, does not touch the structural claim. The machine does not require that every visitor be disappointed. It requires only that the disappointed ones be unable to say so, and that the reported satisfaction of the whole be systematically higher than the experienced satisfaction of the whole. If half the visitors have a good time and half do not, and all of them report a good time, the destination's reputation is exactly as inflated as if none of them had, and the loop turns just the same. The genuinely happy tourist is not a counterexample. He is camouflage for the rest.

I should also be honest about my own position, because a critic will fairly say I am generalizing from my own disappointment. I did not enjoy living there. That is an understatement, and the full account belongs in another article, but I do not want to leave it unsaid: over ten years I was the victim of more crimes than I can easily count, I was subjected to racism in shops, by neighbors, and on the street, and I was stopped and harassed by the local police often enough that it stopped surprising me. None of this changed the fact that the place is physically stunning. I will remember for the rest of my life the mornings hiking in the hills above the bay, the long swims in the sea, the climbs into the foothills of the Alps on a bicycle. The beauty is not the counterfeit. The beauty is real, and it is the bait.

What struck me was how residents responded when I described what had happened to me. Almost without exception they shrugged. This, I was told, is the price of living somewhere so beautiful. It took me a while to recognize that shrug for what it was: the same move my visiting friends made when they got home. The locals had paid dearly to be there, in their case with their whole lives, and they could not afford to conclude that it was not worth it. So they revised the belief instead of the deed, and the beauty of the coast became the answer to every complaint. The loop I have been describing does not only run through the tourists. It runs through everyone who has spent too much to leave. For me, the price was never worth it, and I am one of the few people I met there who was in a position to say so, because I could go.

Boorstin's traveler went out to be changed by the world. The tourist goes out to confirm an image. What I saw was a third figure Boorstin didn't anticipate: the tourist who returns to defend the image, at his own expense, against his own experience, because he has spent too much to do anything else.
