People who see me make a smoothie tend to make the same category error. They see a blender, berries, almond milk, something green and a scoop of protein powder, and they assume I am making a smoothie in the conventional sense: a 300-calorie drink that accompanies breakfast, or stands in for the cookie I would otherwise eat at three in the afternoon. I am not. My smoothies are about 1,100 calories, with 50 to 60 grams of protein, 85 to 100 grams of fat, a substantial amount of fiber, and usually less than 20 grams of digestible carbohydrate. They are lunch.

On most days when I am eating this way, the smoothie is the largest meal I have. Dinner might be a couple of chicken thighs, or half a steak with broccoli and green beans. When I am training hard, with long days of cycling, running or triathlon work, the smoothie becomes additional food instead, because there is a point at which replacing several thousand calories burned on a bicycle with chicken breasts becomes tedious enough that you begin to reconsider your relationship with solid food. The blender solves this surprisingly well.

## **The problem I was actually trying to solve**

I did not set out to invent keto smoothies. I wanted something I could make in five minutes, eat every day without thinking about it, and trust to cover a meaningful share of what I need. It had to carry plenty of protein. It had to contain actual plants, not a multivitamin standing in for them. It needed enough fat and energy that I was not hungry again an hour later. And because I generally eat a ketogenic or very low-carbohydrate diet, it could not achieve any of this by hiding two bananas, a cup of oats and half a bottle of honey under the word "smoothie."

That last trick is remarkably common. A great many things sold as protein smoothies are milkshakes with better public relations. Fruit is nutritious, but four servings of it blended into a drink carry an impressive amount of sugar. Add oat milk, dates, granola and whatever else is fashionable this week, and you have something that may be perfectly good food but has very little to do with staying in ketosis.

These are different. The base is protein powder, frozen spinach and unsweetened almond milk. On top of that go nuts or nut butter, avocado, cream or cream cheese, berries, and whatever produces the flavor I want that day. The result is keto, but it is not nutritionally barren keto, and that distinction matters. It is easy to build a ketogenic diet out of bacon, cheese and mayonnaise. It is also possible to build one around leafy vegetables, avocado, nuts, berries and adequate protein. Both satisfy the carbohydrate arithmetic. They are not the same diet.

Each of these smoothies contains a frankly ridiculous amount of spinach, about 150 grams of densely packed frozen chopped spinach. There is avocado, there are nuts, two of the three have berries, and there is enough protein that the drink is an actual meal rather than flavored fat. Depending on the recipe they also deliver 17 to 21 grams of fiber, which is not trivial on a ketogenic diet.

## **Smoothie one: peanut butter and blackberry**

This is the original, and still the default. It tastes mostly of peanut butter and blackberry. The spinach is present in industrial quantities and, remarkably, contributes almost nothing to the flavor.

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Protein powder | 30 g |
| Frozen chopped spinach | 150 g |
| Unsweetened almond milk | 1 cup |
| Peanut butter | 1/3 cup, about 85 g |
| Double cream | 1/3 cup, about 80 ml |
| Small avocado | 1/2 |
| Frozen blackberries | 8–10 large berries, about 55 g |
| Cocoa powder | 1/2 tsp |
| Cinnamon | A generous sprinkle |
| Ice | 3 cubes |

Put everything in the blender and run it until the spinach is no longer identifiable as a plant. The whole thing comes to roughly:

| | Approximate amount |
|---|---:|
| Calories | **1,150 kcal** |
| Protein | **56 g** |
| Fat | **93 g** |
| Digestible carbohydrate | **19 g** |
| Fiber | **17 g** |

One detail for Americans reading British labels: UK and EU carbohydrate figures already exclude fiber. You do not subtract the fiber a second time to manufacture an even more exciting "net carb" number. The 19 grams above is already the digestible figure.

## **Smoothie two: mocha hazelnut**

Eating the same thing every day is efficient right up until the moment you would rather skip lunch than taste it again. The mocha hazelnut version is the fix. Espresso and cocoa are unusually good at making 150 grams of spinach disappear, and hazelnut butter does the rest.

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Protein powder | 30 g |
| Frozen chopped spinach | 150 g |
| Unsweetened almond milk | 1 cup |
| Hazelnut butter | 70 g |
| Double cream | 60 ml |
| Small avocado | 1/2 |
| Hemp hearts | 30 g |
| Cocoa powder | 1 tsp |
| Espresso | 1 shot |
| Cinnamon | Sprinkle |
| Ice | 3–4 cubes |

| | Approximate amount |
|---|---:|
| Calories | **1,160 kcal** |
| Protein | **53 g** |
| Fat | **100 g** |
| Digestible carbohydrate | **12 g** |
| Fiber | **18 g** |

This is the lowest in carbohydrate of the three. It also tastes much less like something a person drinks because he has made a series of commitments about his health. Espresso, chocolate and hazelnut have been cooperating for centuries. The spinach is an unnoticed passenger.

## **Smoothie three: raspberry cheesecake**

The third version goes the other way. Instead of making the drink darker and nuttier, cream cheese, raspberry, vanilla and lemon make it sharp and slightly sour.

The raspberries account for some of the extra carbohydrate, but they also push the fiber past 20 grams, which for a single meal is a lot.

## **Why so many calories?**

Because calories are food. That sounds obvious, but discussions of smoothies have a strange habit of treating the calorie count as though it were a contamination level. A 1,100-calorie smoothie must be bad, the reasoning goes, because 1,100 is a large number.

That depends rather heavily on what else you eat. If I drank one of these in addition to breakfast, lunch and dinner, it would be a spectacular way to gain weight. But that is not what I am doing. The smoothie *is* lunch. On an ordinary two-meal day, 1,100 calories from the blender followed by some meat and green vegetables at dinner adds up to a perfectly unremarkable daily intake.

When training volume rises, the problem reverses. Spend several hours on a bicycle and getting enough food becomes work. Endurance training burns an enormous amount of energy, and there is only so much chewing I want to do in a day. Liquid calories are useful precisely because they are easy to consume. So the same food does two jobs. In ordinary periods it replaces a meal. In periods of heavy training it supplements one. The recipe did not change. The energy requirement did.

## **But isn't keto terrible for endurance athletes?**

This is where nutritional tribes usually ruin an otherwise useful discussion.

Ketogenic diets reliably increase fat oxidation. That does not mean they have been shown to improve endurance performance, because they haven't. The International Society of Sports Nutrition's [2024 position stand on ketogenic diets](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212571/) concluded that their effect on performance is largely neutral or negative compared with higher-carbohydrate diets, despite the large increase in fat burned during exercise. The same paper allows that the picture for endurance work may depend on training status and on how long the athlete has been adapted, and most of the studies showing a decrement lasted six weeks or less. But high-intensity work remains a legitimate concern, and nobody should pretend otherwise.

None of this should be surprising. Fat is an extraordinary fuel for moving for a long time at a sustainable intensity. Carbohydrate is extraordinarily useful when you want to move very quickly. Biochemistry did not join a diet cult.

So I do not treat ketosis as a moral obligation. For ordinary eating and for much of my training, I like being adapted to a low-carbohydrate diet. It simplifies hunger, simplifies meals, and gives me an enormous internal fuel supply for long, steady efforts. But when the purpose of a session is repeated hard intervals, a time trial or a race, carbohydrate becomes a tool rather than an enemy. The evidence on how much it helps an adapted athlete is thin, mostly small studies and the occasional [single-subject case](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4089926?lng=en), but the physiology is not in dispute. You can spend most of your life with a cordless drill and still recognize that sometimes you need a hammer. The mistake is turning a metabolic state into an identity.

## **Keto without the nutritional cosplay**

What I like about these smoothies is that the ketosis is almost incidental. Remove the word "keto" and look at what is actually in the blender: spinach, avocado, nuts, berries, protein, almond milk, seeds, cocoa.

There is a considerable amount of fat, certainly, and in the versions with a lot of double cream there is a considerable amount of saturated fat. I will not pretend otherwise. If I were optimizing these purely for long-term cardiovascular health rather than for taste, energy and ketosis, shifting some of the cream toward avocado, nuts and seeds would be the obvious place to experiment. But the food is recognizable, and that matters to me, because I have no interest in achieving ketosis by progressively eliminating everything that contains a vitamin.

A restrictive diet should make nutritional quality more important, not less. When carbohydrate is limited, the vegetables that remain have work to do. Spinach, broccoli, green beans, avocado and berries are not decorative concessions to health. They are a substantial part of the diet, and the smoothie is simply an efficient way to deliver them.

## **The thing the blender gets right**

There is a great temptation, when training seriously, to make food complicated. You can count macronutrients to three decimal places. You can buy powders for before the workout, powders for during it, and different powders for the sacred thirty-seven-minute window afterward. You can turn breakfast into an exercise in sports science and end up owning more bottles than a moderately equipped chemistry lab. Some of that has its place. Most of the time I would rather eat food.

The smoothies work because they remove decisions. I know roughly what is in them. I know they contain enough protein to matter, and a good few hundred grams of actual plants. I know they keep carbohydrate low enough to fit the way I usually eat, and carry enough energy to replace a meal or to help cover the cost of serious training. And I know I can make one in five minutes.

There are more sophisticated nutrition strategies. There are very few that survive contact with an ordinary Tuesday as reliably as putting lunch in a blender.
